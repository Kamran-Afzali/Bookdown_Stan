Chapter 11 Measurement Error as a Latent Variable

In applied mental-health research, we routinely treat our predictors as cleaner than they really are. A depression score, an anxiety checklist, a wearable-derived sleep measure, or a self-reported stress scale are all noisy proxies for the underlying constructs we actually care about. Ignoring that noise has consequences: regression coefficients can be attenuated, distorted, or even biased in direction depending on the error structure and the correlations among covariates.

A Bayesian framework offers a principled solution. Instead of treating observed predictors as ground truth, we treat the true latent predictors as unknown quantities and estimate them jointly with the outcome model. This idea is standard in Bayesian measurement-error modeling, where the latent true value is handled as missing data and each observed measurement is modeled conditionally on it.

11.1 Why This Matters

Suppose we want to predict depressive symptom severity at six months using several baseline predictors:

  • latent chronic stress
  • latent sleep disruption
  • latent social isolation

But what we actually observe are noisy proxies:

  • a short self-report stress questionnaire
  • a wearable-derived sleep score
  • a single-item loneliness measure
  • a clinician-rated stress impression
  • a smartphone-based sleep proxy

When those proxies are noisy, plugging them directly into a regression can underestimate the effects of the true predictors — especially under classical measurement error — and can also bias the coefficients of other correlated predictors. That general bias mechanism is well documented in measurement-error and latent-predictor settings.

11.2 The Formal Setup

Let \(Y_i\) be depressive symptom severity for person \(i\). Let the true latent predictors be:

\[ \theta_{i1} = \text{chronic stress}, \quad \theta_{i2} = \text{sleep disruption}, \quad \theta_{i3} = \text{social isolation} \]

The outcome model is:

\[ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta_1 \theta_{i1} + \beta_2 \theta_{i2} + \beta_3 \theta_{i3} + \varepsilon_i^Y, \quad \varepsilon_i^Y \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\sigma_Y^2) \]

Instead of observing \(\theta_{ik}\) directly, we observe multiple noisy indicators \(X_{ij}\). A basic Gaussian measurement model is:

\[ X_{ij} = \lambda_j \theta_{i,g(j)} + \nu_{ij} \]

where \(g(j)\) maps indicator \(j\) to its latent construct, \(\lambda_j\) is the factor loading, and \(\nu_{ij}\) is instrument-specific measurement error. A convenient prior for the latent predictors is multivariate normal:

\[ \boldsymbol{\theta}_i \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \Sigma_\theta) \]

This allows the true mental-health constructs to correlate with one another — a realistic assumption, since stress, sleep disruption, and social isolation tend to co-occur.

11.3 Scenario 1: Uncorrelated Measurement Error

In the simplest case, each indicator has its own independent error term. The DAG looks like this:

library(ggdag)
library(ggplot2)

dag_uncorr <- dagify(
  Y           ~ theta_stress + theta_sleep + theta_iso,
  stress_self ~ theta_stress,
  stress_clin ~ theta_stress,
  sleep_watch ~ theta_sleep,
  sleep_phone ~ theta_sleep,
  lonely_item ~ theta_iso,
  outcome = "Y",
  latent  = c("theta_stress", "theta_sleep", "theta_iso"),
  coords  = list(
    x = c(theta_stress = 0,    theta_sleep = 1,   theta_iso = 2,
          stress_self  = -0.2, stress_clin = 0.2,
          sleep_watch  =  0.8, sleep_phone = 1.2,
          lonely_item  =  2,   Y = 1),
    y = c(theta_stress = 1, theta_sleep = 1, theta_iso = 1,
          stress_self  = 0, stress_clin = 0,
          sleep_watch  = 0, sleep_phone = 0,
          lonely_item  = 0, Y = 2)
  )
)

ggdag(dag_uncorr, text = TRUE, use_labels = "name") +
  theme_dag() +
  ggtitle("Mental Health Model with Uncorrelated Measurement Error")

The interpretation is clean: each observed measure is noisy only because of its own instrument-specific error. Once we condition on the latent construct, one indicator carries no additional information about another. That conditional-independence assumption is the simplifying structure that makes single-factor measurement models tractable.

11.3.1 Measurement Equations

\[ \text{stress\_self}_i = \lambda_1 \theta_{i1} + \nu_{i1}, \quad \nu_{i1} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau_1^2) \]

\[ \text{stress\_clin}_i = \lambda_2 \theta_{i1} + \nu_{i2}, \quad \nu_{i2} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau_2^2) \]

\[ \text{sleep\_watch}_i = \lambda_3 \theta_{i2} + \nu_{i3}, \quad \nu_{i3} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau_3^2) \]

\[ \text{sleep\_phone}_i = \lambda_4 \theta_{i2} + \nu_{i4}, \quad \nu_{i4} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau_4^2) \]

\[ \text{lonely\_item}_i = \lambda_5 \theta_{i3} + \nu_{i5}, \quad \nu_{i5} \sim \mathcal{N}(0,\tau_5^2) \]

This is the cleanest version to fit and the easiest to communicate. It is generally the right starting point when the primary concern is attenuation from noisy predictors rather than residual dependence between instruments.

11.4 Scenario 2: Correlated Measurement Error

Now suppose the two sleep indicators share method artifacts. Both the wearable and the smartphone proxy may be sensitive to device non-wear, irregular charging, or common preprocessing pipelines. Even after conditioning on true sleep disruption, these indicators may still covary — not because of the construct, but because of shared method variance.

dag_corr <- dagify(
  Y           ~ theta_stress + theta_sleep + theta_iso,
  stress_self ~ theta_stress,
  stress_clin ~ theta_stress,
  sleep_watch ~ theta_sleep + e_sleep_method,
  sleep_phone ~ theta_sleep + e_sleep_method,
  lonely_item ~ theta_iso,
  latent = c("theta_stress", "theta_sleep", "theta_iso", "e_sleep_method"),
  coords = list(
    x = c(theta_stress = 0,   theta_sleep = 1,   theta_iso = 2,
          e_sleep_method = 1,
          stress_self = -0.2, stress_clin = 0.2,
          sleep_watch =  0.8, sleep_phone = 1.2,
          lonely_item =  2,   Y = 1),
    y = c(theta_stress = 1, theta_sleep = 1.4, theta_iso = 1,
          e_sleep_method = 0.8,
          stress_self = 0, stress_clin = 0,
          sleep_watch = 0, sleep_phone = 0,
          lonely_item = 0, Y = 2.2)
  )
)

ggdag(dag_corr, text = TRUE, use_labels = "name") +
  theme_dag() +
  ggtitle("Mental Health Model with Correlated Measurement Error")

This shared-error source can also be encoded as a residual covariance directly, but the DAG representation is often more intuitive: it makes the causal story explicit rather than absorbing the covariance into an unexplained term.

11.4.1 Measurement Equations

For the sleep indicators, a latent method factor \(\eta_i\) induces the additional covariance:

\[ \text{sleep\_watch}_i = \lambda_3 \theta_{i2} + \delta_1 \eta_i + \nu_{i3} \]

\[ \text{sleep\_phone}_i = \lambda_4 \theta_{i2} + \delta_2 \eta_i + \nu_{i4} \]

\[ \eta_i \sim \mathcal{N}(0,1) \]

This formulation is generally more stable and more interpretable than simply adding an unconstrained covariance term, because it narrates why the indicators covary beyond the construct itself.

11.5 Simulating a Mental-Health Example

We simulate a study with three correlated latent predictors and five noisy indicators. The outcome is continuous depression severity at follow-up.

set.seed(123)
n <- 600

Sigma_theta <- matrix(c(
  1.00, 0.45, 0.35,
  0.45, 1.00, 0.30,
  0.35, 0.30, 1.00
), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)

library(MASS)
theta        <- MASS::mvrnorm(n, mu = rep(0, 3), Sigma = Sigma_theta)
theta_stress <- theta[, 1]
theta_sleep  <- theta[, 2]
theta_iso    <- theta[, 3]

beta0   <- 0.0;  beta1 <- 0.9;  beta2 <- 0.7;  beta3 <- 0.8
sigma_y <- 1.2

# Uncorrelated measurement error
stress_self <- 1.0 * theta_stress + rnorm(n, 0, 0.9)
stress_clin <- 0.8 * theta_stress + rnorm(n, 0, 0.7)
sleep_watch <- 1.0 * theta_sleep  + rnorm(n, 0, 0.8)
sleep_phone <- 0.7 * theta_sleep  + rnorm(n, 0, 1.0)
lonely_item <- 1.0 * theta_iso    + rnorm(n, 0, 1.1)

y <- beta0 + beta1 * theta_stress + beta2 * theta_sleep + beta3 * theta_iso +
     rnorm(n, 0, sigma_y)

dat <- data.frame(y, stress_self, stress_clin, sleep_watch, sleep_phone, lonely_item)

This data-generating process mirrors the core Bayesian measurement-error principle: the true predictors generate the observed noisy proxies, and the outcome depends on the true predictors — not on the imperfect measurements.

11.6 Stan Model: Uncorrelated Error

The following Stan model estimates the latent predictors and the regression coefficients simultaneously.

stan_me_uncorr <- "
data {
  int<lower=1> N;
  vector[N] y;
  vector[N] stress_self;
  vector[N] stress_clin;
  vector[N] sleep_watch;
  vector[N] sleep_phone;
  vector[N] lonely_item;
}
parameters {
  real            beta0;
  vector[3]       beta;           // FIXED: was vector beta; — dimension required
  real<lower=0>   sigma_y;

  vector[N] theta_stress;
  vector[N] theta_sleep;
  vector[N] theta_iso;

  real<lower=0> lambda_ss;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sc;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sw;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sp;
  real<lower=0> lambda_li;

  real<lower=0> tau_ss;
  real<lower=0> tau_sc;
  real<lower=0> tau_sw;
  real<lower=0> tau_sp;
  real<lower=0> tau_li;

  cholesky_factor_corr[3] L_Omega;   // FIXED: missing dimension [3]
  vector<lower=0>[3]      L_sigma;   // FIXED: missing dimension [3]
}
transformed parameters {
  matrix[3,3] L_Sigma = diag_pre_multiply(L_sigma, L_Omega);
}
model {
  beta0   ~ normal(0, 2);
  beta    ~ normal(0, 1.5);
  sigma_y ~ exponential(1);

  lambda_ss ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sc ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sw ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sp ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_li ~ normal(1, 0.5);

  tau_ss ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sc ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sw ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sp ~ exponential(1);
  tau_li ~ exponential(1);

  L_sigma ~ exponential(1);
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2);

  // Joint prior on latent predictors
  for (i in 1:N) {
    [theta_stress[i], theta_sleep[i], theta_iso[i]]' ~
      multi_normal_cholesky(rep_vector(0, 3), L_Sigma);
  }

  // Measurement model
  stress_self ~ normal(lambda_ss * theta_stress, tau_ss);
  stress_clin ~ normal(lambda_sc * theta_stress, tau_sc);
  sleep_watch ~ normal(lambda_sw * theta_sleep,  tau_sw);
  sleep_phone ~ normal(lambda_sp * theta_sleep,  tau_sp);
  lonely_item ~ normal(lambda_li * theta_iso,    tau_li);

  // Outcome model — FIXED: was beta * theta_..., now indexed correctly
  y ~ normal(beta0
             + beta[1] * theta_stress
             + beta[2] * theta_sleep
             + beta[3] * theta_iso,
             sigma_y);
}
generated quantities {
  corr_matrix[3] Omega                          // FIXED: missing dimension [3]
    = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
}
"
writeLines(stan_me_uncorr, "stan_me_uncorr.stan")

The posterior for each beta[k] now reflects genuine uncertainty about the true construct, rather than assuming the observed proxy is exact. That is the central inferential advantage of Bayesian measurement-error modeling: uncertainty about the latent predictor propagates through to uncertainty about its regression coefficient.

11.7 Stan Model: Correlated Error

Now we add a latent method factor for the two sleep indicators. The fixes from the uncorrelated model apply here as well, plus a generated quantities block is added.

stan_me_corr <- "
data {
  int<lower=1> N;
  vector[N] y;
  vector[N] stress_self;
  vector[N] stress_clin;
  vector[N] sleep_watch;
  vector[N] sleep_phone;
  vector[N] lonely_item;
}
parameters {
  real          beta0;
  vector[3]     beta;             // FIXED: dimension required
  real<lower=0> sigma_y;

  vector[N] theta_stress;
  vector[N] theta_sleep;
  vector[N] theta_iso;
  vector[N] eta_sleep_method;

  real<lower=0> lambda_ss;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sc;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sw;
  real<lower=0> lambda_sp;
  real<lower=0> lambda_li;

  real delta_sw;
  real delta_sp;

  real<lower=0> tau_ss;
  real<lower=0> tau_sc;
  real<lower=0> tau_sw;
  real<lower=0> tau_sp;
  real<lower=0> tau_li;

  cholesky_factor_corr[3] L_Omega;  // FIXED: missing dimension [3]
  vector<lower=0>[3]      L_sigma;  // FIXED: missing dimension [3]
}
transformed parameters {
  matrix[3,3] L_Sigma = diag_pre_multiply(L_sigma, L_Omega);
}
model {
  beta0   ~ normal(0, 2);
  beta    ~ normal(0, 1.5);
  sigma_y ~ exponential(1);

  lambda_ss ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sc ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sw ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_sp ~ normal(1, 0.5);
  lambda_li ~ normal(1, 0.5);

  delta_sw ~ normal(0, 0.7);
  delta_sp ~ normal(0, 0.7);

  tau_ss ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sc ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sw ~ exponential(1);
  tau_sp ~ exponential(1);
  tau_li ~ exponential(1);

  L_sigma ~ exponential(1);
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2);

  eta_sleep_method ~ normal(0, 1);

  for (i in 1:N) {
    [theta_stress[i], theta_sleep[i], theta_iso[i]]' ~
      multi_normal_cholesky(rep_vector(0, 3), L_Sigma);
  }

  stress_self ~ normal(lambda_ss * theta_stress, tau_ss);
  stress_clin ~ normal(lambda_sc * theta_stress, tau_sc);
  sleep_watch ~ normal(lambda_sw * theta_sleep + delta_sw * eta_sleep_method, tau_sw);
  sleep_phone ~ normal(lambda_sp * theta_sleep + delta_sp * eta_sleep_method, tau_sp);
  lonely_item ~ normal(lambda_li * theta_iso, tau_li);

  // FIXED: beta indexed correctly
  y ~ normal(beta0
             + beta[1] * theta_stress
             + beta[2] * theta_sleep
             + beta[3] * theta_iso,
             sigma_y);
}
generated quantities {
  // FIXED: block was absent; added with correct dimension
  corr_matrix[3] Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
}
"
writeLines(stan_me_corr, "stan_me_corr.stan")

This version is appropriate when two or more indicators share method variance that is not part of the underlying construct — common examples in mental-health data include two digital phenotyping variables from the same phone pipeline, two subscales from the same questionnaire, or two ratings from the same informant.

11.8 Choosing Between the Two Scenarios

Criterion Uncorrelated error Correlated error
Indicators share an instrument or pipeline No Yes
Residuals independent after conditioning on latent ✓ Assumed ✗ Violated
Model complexity Lower Higher
Risk of ignoring Attenuation bias Inflated apparent construct signal

Ignoring correlated measurement error is particularly consequential: the model may absorb shared method variance into the latent construct itself, making a trait appear stronger or cleaner than it truly is. A latent method factor makes that source of dependence explicit and separable.

11.9 What Changes in Interpretation

Once the model is fit, the posterior means of the latent predictors are not “true scores” in any magical sense. They are shrinkage estimates — they combine prior structure, cross-indicator information, and the outcome model, exactly as Bayesian latent-variable frameworks intend.

That is a feature. A participant with a very high stress self-report but a moderate clinician score and a modest depression outcome will not be forced into an extreme stress estimate. The posterior compromises across those sources of evidence, and that compromise is precisely what principled uncertainty propagation is supposed to produce.

11.10 Practical Cautions

Identification. Latent-variable models require scale constraints. In practice, either fix one loading per factor to 1 and estimate the latent variance freely, or fix the latent variance to 1 and estimate all loadings. Without such a constraint, the model can rescale the latent variable and the loadings in tandem, leaving the likelihood unchanged but the parameters non-identified.

Assumption dependence. The model estimates latent predictors under assumptions about normality, conditional independence, and measurement structure. It does not recover truth from noise without those assumptions. That is the same caution that applies across Bayesian latent-variable and measurement-error modeling more broadly: a well-specified model is powerful, but a misspecified one can produce posteriors that are confidently wrong.
