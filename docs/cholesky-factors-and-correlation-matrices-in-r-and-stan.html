Chapter 22 Cholesky Factors and Correlation Matrices in R and Stan

The Cholesky decomposition is a fundamental matrix factorization technique that plays a crucial role in computational statistics, particularly in Bayesian modeling of multivariate distributions. In Stan, the cholesky_factor_corr type provides an efficient and numerically stable way to work with correlation matrices, making it essential for modeling correlated outcomes, random effects, and multivariate regression problems. This article explores the mathematical foundations, practical implementations, and applications of Cholesky factors in both R and Stan, with particular emphasis on their use in modern Bayesian workflows.

22.1 Introduction to Cholesky Decomposition

22.1.1 What is Cholesky Decomposition?

The Cholesky decomposition is a specialized matrix factorization that applies to symmetric positive definite matrices. For any such matrix A, the Cholesky decomposition expresses it as the product of a lower triangular matrix L with its transpose. Formally, we write this relationship as \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\), where L is called the Cholesky factor. This lower triangular matrix has positive diagonal elements, and this decomposition is unique for any given positive definite matrix.

The Cholesky factor possesses several key characteristics that make it particularly useful in statistical computing. First, being lower triangular means all elements above the diagonal are exactly zero, which dramatically reduces the number of parameters that need to be estimated or stored. Second, the requirement that all diagonal elements be strictly positive ensures the decomposition exists only for matrices that are genuinely positive definite, providing a natural diagnostic for the validity of covariance structures. This uniqueness property is analogous to taking the “square root” of a matrix, though in a matrix-algebraic rather than element-wise sense.

Consider a concrete example with a \(3 \times 3\) covariance matrix that might arise in a health study measuring three correlated biomarkers:

\[ \mathbf{\Sigma} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 & 1 \\ 2 & 3 & 1.5 \\ 1 & 1.5 & 2 \end{bmatrix} \]

Its Cholesky factor is:

\[ \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 & 0 \\ 1 & 1.414 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.707 & 1.225 \end{bmatrix} \]

You can verify this relationship by computing the matrix product \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\), which will exactly reconstruct the original covariance matrix Σ. This factorization essentially decomposes the complex correlation structure into a sequence of conditional relationships encoded in the lower triangular structure.

22.1.2 Why Use Cholesky Decomposition?

The Cholesky decomposition offers several compelling advantages over alternative matrix factorization methods. From a computational perspective, the algorithm requires approximately \(\frac{n^3}{6}\) operations for an \(n \times n\) matrix, which is exactly half the work required by the more general LU decomposition. This efficiency becomes critical when working with large covariance matrices in hierarchical models or when performing repeated matrix operations during MCMC sampling, where the same factorization may be needed thousands or millions of times.

Beyond computational speed, the Cholesky decomposition provides exceptional numerical stability without requiring pivoting operations, which involve strategically swapping rows or columns to avoid numerical issues. This stability makes it far more reliable than general matrix decompositions for the symmetric positive definite matrices that naturally arise in statistical applications. The algorithm’s failure itself serves as a diagnostic tool—if the Cholesky decomposition fails to complete, you immediately know the matrix is not positive definite, which would indicate a fundamental problem with your covariance structure.

Perhaps most importantly for statistical applications, the Cholesky factor enables remarkably efficient sampling from multivariate normal distributions. If you have \(n\) independent standard normal variables \(\mathbf{z} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), you can generate samples from any desired multivariate normal distribution \(N(\boldsymbol{\mu}, \mathbf{\Sigma})\) through the simple transformation \(\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}\), where L is the Cholesky factor of Σ. This transformation is both exact and efficient, requiring only a matrix-vector multiplication rather than the matrix inversion or eigendecomposition that alternative methods would require.

22.1.3 Cholesky Factors in Stan

Stan provides two specialized variable types specifically designed for Cholesky factors, recognizing their central importance in Bayesian computation. The type cholesky_factor_cov[N] represents the Cholesky factor of an \(N \times N\) covariance matrix, while cholesky_factor_corr[N] represents the Cholesky factor of an \(N \times N\) correlation matrix. This distinction is important because these matrices have fundamentally different constraints on their structure.

Covariance matrices have arbitrary positive values on the diagonal representing the variances of each variable, while correlation matrices have exactly 1 on every diagonal element because they represent standardized relationships. This structural difference manifests in the Cholesky factors as well. For correlation matrices, the Cholesky factor has the special property that each row has unit Euclidean norm, ensuring that when you reconstruct the matrix through \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\), the diagonal elements will all equal one.

In computational health research and behavioral science, you’ll often work with correlation matrices when modeling the correlation structure of random effects or multivariate outcomes, making cholesky_factor_corr particularly relevant. For instance, when modeling patient-level random effects in a multi-site clinical trial, you might allow each site to have correlated random intercepts and slopes. The correlation between these random effects captures whether sites with higher baseline levels also tend to have steeper treatment responses, and this structure is naturally parameterized using the Cholesky factor of a correlation matrix.

22.2 Mathematical Foundation

22.2.1 The Cholesky Decomposition Algorithm

The Cholesky decomposition can be computed iteratively using the Cholesky-Banachiewicz algorithm, which builds up the lower triangular matrix L column by column. For a symmetric positive definite matrix A, the algorithm computes diagonal elements using the formula \(L_{jj} = \sqrt{a_{jj} - \sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1} L_{j\ell}^2}\), which represents taking the square root of the diagonal entry after subtracting the sum of squares of all previously computed elements in that row. Off-diagonal elements are computed using \(L_{ij} = \frac{1}{L_{jj}} \left(a_{ij} - \sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1} L_{i\ell}L_{j\ell}\right)\) for \(i > j\), which divides the residual covariance by the just-computed diagonal element.

The algorithm proceeds systematically. For each column \(j\) from 1 to \(n\), we first compute the diagonal element by calculating \(s = a_{jj} - \sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1} L_{j\ell}^2\). If this quantity is less than or equal to zero, the algorithm must terminate with an error indicating the matrix is not positive definite—the square root would be undefined or purely imaginary. If \(s\) is positive, we set \(L_{jj} = \sqrt{s}\) and proceed to compute the off-diagonal elements in column \(j\). For each row \(k\) from \(j+1\) to \(n\), we compute \(L_{kj} = \frac{1}{L_{jj}}\left(a_{kj} - \sum_{\ell=1}^{j-1} L_{k\ell}L_{j\ell}\right)\).

Let’s walk through a concrete example to illustrate this process. Consider the \(2 \times 2\) matrix:

\[ \mathbf{A} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix} \]

In the first step with \(j=1\), we compute the first diagonal element as \(L_{11} = \sqrt{a_{11}} = \sqrt{4} = 2\). Then we compute the first off-diagonal element as \(L_{21} = \frac{a_{21}}{L_{11}} = \frac{2}{2} = 1\). Moving to the second step with \(j=2\), we compute \(L_{22} = \sqrt{a_{22} - L_{21}^2} = \sqrt{3 - 1^2} = \sqrt{2} \approx 1.414\). This gives us the complete Cholesky factor:

\[ \mathbf{L} = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1.414 \end{bmatrix} \]

We can verify this result by computing the product \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T = \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 0 \\ 1 & 1.414 \end{bmatrix} \begin{bmatrix} 2 & 1 \\ 0 & 1.414 \end{bmatrix} = \begin{bmatrix} 4 & 2 \\ 2 & 3 \end{bmatrix}\), which exactly recovers our original matrix A.

22.2.2 Correlation Matrices and Their Cholesky Factors

A correlation matrix Ω represents a special type of covariance matrix with specific structural constraints. It must be symmetric, meaning \(\Omega_{ij} = \Omega_{ji}\), and all diagonal elements must equal exactly 1, representing the fact that any variable has perfect correlation with itself. Off-diagonal elements are correlations constrained to lie in the interval \([-1, 1]\), and the entire matrix must be positive semi-definite to represent a valid correlation structure. In practice, if there are no perfect correlations among variables, the matrix will be strictly positive definite.

The relationship between covariance and correlation matrices provides important intuition. If Σ is a covariance matrix with standard deviations \(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n\) on the diagonal, we can extract the correlation matrix through the transformation \(\mathbf{\Omega} = \mathbf{D}^{-1}\mathbf{\Sigma}\mathbf{D}^{-1}\), where \(\mathbf{D} = \text{diag}(\sigma_1, \ldots, \sigma_n)\) is a diagonal matrix of standard deviations. This operation standardizes each variable to unit variance while preserving the correlation structure.

When we compute the Cholesky factor of a correlation matrix, the resulting lower triangular matrix \(\mathbf{L}_\Omega\) has a special property: each row has Euclidean norm equal to one. This follows directly from the fact that diagonal elements of Ω equal one, because when we compute \(\mathbf{\Omega} = \mathbf{L}_\Omega \mathbf{L}_\Omega^T\), the diagonal elements are \(\Omega_{ii} = \sum_{j=1}^i L_{ij}^2\), which must equal 1.

Consider a \(3 \times 3\) correlation matrix with pairwise correlations \(\rho_{12} = 0.5\), \(\rho_{13} = 0.3\), and \(\rho_{23} = 0.4\):

\[ \mathbf{\Omega} = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0.5 & 0.3 \\ 0.5 & 1 & 0.4 \\ 0.3 & 0.4 & 1 \end{bmatrix} \]

The Cholesky factor of this correlation matrix is:

\[ \mathbf{L}_\Omega = \begin{bmatrix} 1 & 0 & 0 \\ 0.5 & 0.866 & 0 \\ 0.3 & 0.289 & 0.908 \end{bmatrix} \]

We can verify the unit norm property for each row. The first row has norm \(\sqrt{1^2} = 1\), the second row has norm \(\sqrt{0.5^2 + 0.866^2} = \sqrt{0.25 + 0.75} = 1\), and the third row has norm \(\sqrt{0.3^2 + 0.289^2 + 0.908^2} = \sqrt{0.09 + 0.084 + 0.824} \approx 1\). This property is fundamental to how Stan parameterizes correlation matrices through their Cholesky factors.

22.2.3 The LKJ Distribution

In Bayesian modeling, we need a prior distribution for correlation matrices that captures our prior beliefs about the strength of correlations. The LKJ (Lewandowski-Kurowicka-Joe) distribution has become the standard choice for this purpose, offering both theoretical elegance and practical flexibility. For a \(K \times K\) correlation matrix Ω, the LKJ density is proportional to the determinant of the matrix raised to the power \(\eta - 1\), written formally as \(p(\mathbf{\Omega} \mid \eta) \propto \det(\mathbf{\Omega})^{\eta - 1}\), where \(\eta > 0\) is a shape parameter that controls the distribution’s concentration.

The interpretation of the shape parameter \(\eta\) is intuitive and has important implications for prior specification. When \(\eta = 1\), the LKJ distribution becomes uniform over all valid correlation matrices, meaning every possible correlation structure is equally likely a priori. This might seem like a natural “uninformative” choice, but it actually places substantial prior mass on strong correlations, which may not reflect genuine prior ignorance in many applications.

When \(\eta > 1\), the distribution favors correlation matrices closer to the identity matrix, effectively expressing a prior belief that variables are weakly correlated. As \(\eta\) increases, this concentration becomes stronger—the distribution places more and more probability mass near zero correlation. A common weakly informative choice is \(\eta = 2\), which expresses mild skepticism about strong correlations without ruling them out entirely. This choice often performs well across diverse applications, allowing the data to dominate the posterior when correlations are genuinely present while preventing the model from overfitting to spurious correlations in small samples.

Conversely, when \(\eta < 1\), the distribution favors extreme correlations closer to \(\pm 1\). This unusual choice is rarely used in practice unless you have strong prior evidence that variables are highly correlated. The behavior at the boundaries is worth noting: as \(\eta\) approaches zero, the distribution concentrates mass at the extreme correlations, while very large values of \(\eta\) concentrate mass at the identity matrix.

For a \(2 \times 2\) correlation matrix with a single correlation parameter \(\rho\), the LKJ prior’s behavior is particularly easy to visualize. With \(\eta = 1\), the correlation \(\rho\) is uniformly distributed on \([-1, 1]\). With \(\eta = 2\), the distribution of \(\rho\) takes on a beta-like shape peaked at zero. With \(\eta = 4\), this concentration around zero becomes even more pronounced, expressing a strong prior belief that the variables are nearly independent.

Stan strongly recommends using the Cholesky factor parameterization through cholesky_factor_corr rather than working with correlation matrices directly. This recommendation stems from several important computational considerations. First, the Cholesky factor representation avoids numerical issues that can arise with nearly singular correlation matrices, where some eigenvalues are close to zero. Second, Stan’s MCMC sampler operates more efficiently in the unconstrained space that the Cholesky factor lives in after appropriate transformations. Third, operations on Cholesky factors typically have \(O(n^2)\) computational complexity compared to \(O(n^3)\) for full matrices, which becomes crucial when the dimension of the correlation matrix is large. Finally, the cholesky_factor_corr type automatically ensures all required constraints are satisfied, eliminating a common source of programming errors.

22.3 Properties and Applications

22.3.1 Properties of Cholesky Decomposition

The uniqueness property of the Cholesky decomposition provides both theoretical clarity and practical benefits. For a given symmetric positive definite matrix, there exists exactly one Cholesky decomposition where the diagonal elements of L are positive. This uniqueness means that the Cholesky factor provides a canonical representation of the covariance structure, ensuring that different optimization or sampling routines will converge to the same solution (up to numerical precision).

The Cholesky decomposition provides an efficient approach to solving linear systems of equations. When you need to solve \(\mathbf{A}\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{b}\) where A is symmetric positive definite, you can decompose \(\mathbf{A} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\) and then solve two triangular systems sequentially. First, solve \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{b}\) for y using forward substitution, then solve \(\mathbf{L}^T\mathbf{x} = \mathbf{y}\) for x using backward substitution. This approach is substantially faster and more numerically stable than computing the inverse of A directly and then multiplying by b.

Computing the determinant of a matrix becomes trivial once you have its Cholesky decomposition. The determinant of A equals the square of the determinant of L, which in turn equals the product of its diagonal elements. Formally, \(\det(\mathbf{A}) = \det(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T) = \det(\mathbf{L})^2 = \left(\prod_{i=1}^n L_{ii}\right)^2\). This means you simply multiply the diagonal elements of L together and square the result, avoiding the \(O(n^3)\) operations required by general determinant algorithms.

While explicit matrix inversion is rarely needed in modern statistical computing (and is often a sign of inefficient code), when an inverse is genuinely required, the Cholesky decomposition provides an efficient route. The inverse of A can be expressed as \(\mathbf{A}^{-1} = (\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T)^{-1} = (\mathbf{L}^T)^{-1}\mathbf{L}^{-1}\). Because L is triangular, computing its inverse is relatively straightforward and numerically stable compared to inverting a general matrix.

22.3.2 Applications in Statistical Modeling

The most fundamental application of Cholesky decomposition in statistics is efficient sampling from multivariate normal distributions. Suppose you need to generate samples from a multivariate normal distribution with mean vector μ and covariance matrix Σ. The standard approach computes the Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\), generates independent standard normal variables \(\mathbf{z} \sim N(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})\), and then transforms them through \(\mathbf{y} = \boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}\). The mathematical justification is straightforward: the covariance of y equals \(\text{Cov}(\boldsymbol{\mu} + \mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}) = \mathbf{L}\text{Cov}(\mathbf{z})\mathbf{L}^T = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{I}\mathbf{L}^T = \mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T = \mathbf{\Sigma}\), exactly as desired. The computational advantage is substantial: generating \(n\) independent standard normals is trivial, and the matrix-vector multiplication \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{z}\) requires only \(O(n^2)\) operations.

In hierarchical models, correlated random effects naturally arise when different parameters vary together across groups. Consider a multi-site clinical trial where each site has a random intercept capturing its baseline level and a random slope capturing its treatment effect. These random effects are often correlated—sites with higher baseline disease severity might show larger or smaller treatment responses depending on the intervention mechanism. The model specifies that for site \(j\), the random effects vector \(\begin{bmatrix} b_{0j} \\ b_{1j} \end{bmatrix}\) follows a bivariate normal distribution with mean zero and covariance matrix Σ_b.

This covariance matrix can be decomposed as \(\mathbf{\Sigma}_b = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{D}\), where D is a diagonal matrix of standard deviations and Ω is the correlation matrix. In Stan, the most efficient implementation strategy models Ω using cholesky_factor_corr with an LKJ prior, models the standard deviations separately (often with half-Cauchy or half-normal priors), and then combines them to form the Cholesky factor of the full covariance matrix through \(\mathbf{L}_\Sigma = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega\). This decomposition separates the magnitude of variation (captured by the standard deviations) from the pattern of correlation (captured by Ω), which often reflects the natural structure of prior knowledge and makes prior specification more intuitive.

Multivariate regression extends standard regression to settings with multiple correlated outcome variables. The model specifies that each observation’s outcome vector Y_i follows a multivariate normal distribution with mean given by the linear predictor X_iB and residual covariance Σ. Instead of modeling Σ directly, we decompose it as \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{D}\), giving a Cholesky factor \(\mathbf{L}_\Sigma = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega\). This parameterization clearly separates the marginal variances of each outcome (the elements of D) from their correlations (captured by Ω), making both prior specification and posterior interpretation more straightforward.

Seemingly unrelated regression (SUR) provides another important application of Cholesky factors. SUR models simultaneously fit multiple regression equations where the residual errors are correlated across equations but observations remain independent. A classic example in health research would be jointly modeling systolic and diastolic blood pressure as functions of patient characteristics. While these are different outcomes with potentially different predictors and effect sizes, their residual errors are inevitably correlated—if a patient’s systolic blood pressure is higher than their characteristics would predict, their diastolic pressure is likely elevated too. The Cholesky parameterization allows efficient estimation of this residual correlation structure while maintaining computational efficiency even when the number of equations grows large.

22.4 Implementation in R

22.4.1 Basic Cholesky Decomposition

R provides the chol() function for computing Cholesky decompositions. Let’s start with a basic example using a symmetric positive definite matrix:

# Define a symmetric positive definite matrix
A <- matrix(c(4, 2, 1,
              2, 3, 1.5,
              1, 1.5, 2), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)

print(A)
#      [,1] [,2] [,3]
# [1,]  4.0  2.0  1.0
# [2,]  2.0  3.0  1.5
# [3,]  1.0  1.5  2.0

# Compute Cholesky decomposition
# Note: chol() returns UPPER triangular matrix by default
L_upper <- chol(A)
print(L_upper)
#      [,1]      [,2]      [,3]
# [1,] 2.000000  1.000000  0.500000
# [2,] 0.000000  1.414214  0.707107
# [3,] 0.000000  0.000000  1.224745

# Get lower triangular form (Stan uses lower triangular)
L <- t(L_upper)
print(L)
#      [,1]      [,2]      [,3]
# [1,] 2.000000  0.000000  0.000000
# [2,] 1.000000  1.414214  0.000000
# [3,] 0.500000  0.707107  1.224745

# Verify: L %*% t(L) should equal A
reconstructed <- L %*% t(L)
print(reconstructed)
#      [,1] [,2] [,3]
# [1,]  4.0  2.0  1.0
# [2,]  2.0  3.0  1.5
# [3,]  1.0  1.5  2.0

# Check equality (accounting for numerical precision)
all.equal(A, reconstructed)
# [1] TRUE

An important implementation detail here is that R’s chol() function returns an upper triangular matrix by default, while Stan uses lower triangular matrices throughout its implementation. You need to transpose the result if you’re comparing with Stan output or implementing algorithms that expect the lower triangular form.

22.4.2 Working with Correlation Matrices

Correlation matrices have special structure that the Cholesky decomposition must preserve:

# Create a correlation matrix
# Correlations: rho_12 = 0.6, rho_13 = 0.3, rho_23 = 0.5
Omega <- matrix(c(1.0, 0.6, 0.3,
                  0.6, 1.0, 0.5,
                  0.3, 0.5, 1.0), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)

print(Omega)
#      [,1] [,2] [,3]
# [1,]  1.0  0.6  0.3
# [2,]  0.6  1.0  0.5
# [3,]  0.3  0.5  1.0

# Check if positive definite
# All eigenvalues should be positive
eigenvalues <- eigen(Omega)$values
print(eigenvalues)
# [1] 1.8532033 0.9155961 0.2312006

all(eigenvalues > 0)
# [1] TRUE

# Compute Cholesky factor of correlation matrix
L_omega_upper <- chol(Omega)
L_omega <- t(L_omega_upper)

print(L_omega)
#      [,1]      [,2]      [,3]
# [1,] 1.0000000 0.0000000 0.0000000
# [2,] 0.6000000 0.8000000 0.0000000
# [3,] 0.3000000 0.4000000 0.8246211

# Verify each row has norm 1 (property of correlation matrix Cholesky factor)
row_norms <- apply(L_omega, 1, function(row) sqrt(sum(row^2)))
print(row_norms)
# [1] 1 1 1

# Reconstruct correlation matrix
Omega_reconstructed <- L_omega %*% t(L_omega)
all.equal(Omega, Omega_reconstructed)
# [1] TRUE

The verification that each row of the Cholesky factor has unit norm confirms we’re working with a correlation matrix rather than a general covariance matrix. This property is fundamental to how Stan internally represents and manipulates correlation matrices.

22.4.3 Sampling from Multivariate Normal Using Cholesky

The Cholesky decomposition provides the most efficient method for generating multivariate normal samples:

# Generate samples from MVN using Cholesky factor
set.seed(123)

# Parameters
mu <- c(10, 20, 30)  # Mean vector
Sigma <- matrix(c(4.0, 2.0, 1.0,
                  2.0, 3.0, 1.5,
                  1.0, 1.5, 2.0), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)

# Cholesky factor
L <- t(chol(Sigma))

# Number of samples
n_samples <- 1000

# Generate independent standard normals
Z <- matrix(rnorm(n_samples * 3), nrow = n_samples, ncol = 3)

# Transform to MVN samples: Y = mu + Z %*% t(L)
# (Using t(L) because we're post-multiplying row vectors)
Y <- sweep(Z %*% t(L), 2, mu, "+")

# Verify sample mean
sample_mean <- colMeans(Y)
print(sample_mean)
# [1]  9.982158 19.975441 29.961883
# Very close to (10, 20, 30)

# Verify sample covariance
sample_cov <- cov(Y)
print(sample_cov)
#           [,1]      [,2]      [,3]
# [1,] 3.9915822 1.9743334 0.9891376
# [2,] 1.9743334 3.0319438 1.4931773
# [3,] 0.9891376 1.4931773 2.0077803
# Very close to Sigma

print(Sigma)
#      [,1] [,2] [,3]
# [1,]  4.0  2.0  1.0
# [2,]  2.0  3.0  1.5
# [3,]  1.0  1.5  2.0

The close agreement between sample moments and population parameters demonstrates the correctness of this sampling approach. With a thousand samples, sampling variability accounts for the small discrepancies we observe.

22.4.4 Converting Between Covariance and Correlation

Understanding the relationship between covariance and correlation matrices, and their respective Cholesky factors, is essential for working with Stan’s parameterization:

# Start with a covariance matrix
Sigma <- matrix(c(4.0, 2.0, 1.0,
                  2.0, 3.0, 1.5,
                  1.0, 1.5, 2.0), nrow = 3, byrow = TRUE)

# Extract standard deviations (square root of diagonal)
sds <- sqrt(diag(Sigma))
print(sds)
# [1] 2.000000 1.732051 1.414214

# Compute correlation matrix
# Omega = D^(-1) Sigma D^(-1) where D = diag(sds)
D_inv <- diag(1 / sds)
Omega <- D_inv %*% Sigma %*% D_inv

print(Omega)
#           [,1]      [,2]      [,3]
# [1,] 1.0000000 0.5773503 0.3535534
# [2,] 0.5773503 1.0000000 0.6123724
# [3,] 0.3535534 0.6123724 1.0000000

# Verify diagonal is all 1s
diag(Omega)
# [1] 1 1 1

# Convert back to covariance
# Sigma = D Omega D
D <- diag(sds)
Sigma_reconstructed <- D %*% Omega %*% D

all.equal(Sigma, Sigma_reconstructed)
# [1] TRUE

# Cholesky factors
# For covariance: L_Sigma = D * L_Omega
L_omega <- t(chol(Omega))
L_sigma_from_parts <- D %*% L_omega

# Direct Cholesky of covariance
L_sigma_direct <- t(chol(Sigma))

# Should be equal
all.equal(L_sigma_from_parts, L_sigma_direct)
# [1] TRUE

This example demonstrates the key relationship that Stan exploits: the Cholesky factor of a covariance matrix can be constructed by pre-multiplying the Cholesky factor of the correlation matrix by the diagonal matrix of standard deviations. This factorization allows separate priors on scales and correlations.

22.5 Implementation in Stan

22.5.1 Basic Cholesky Factor Model

Here’s a minimal Stan model demonstrating the use of cholesky_factor_corr:

data {
  int<lower=1> K;      // Dimension of correlation matrix
  real<lower=0> eta;   // LKJ shape parameter
}

parameters {
  cholesky_factor_corr[K] L_Omega;  // Cholesky factor of correlation matrix
}

model {
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(eta); // LKJ prior on Cholesky factor
}

generated quantities {
  corr_matrix[K] Omega;              // Reconstructed correlation matrix
  Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);  // Omega = L_Omega * L_Omega'
}

This model samples directly from the LKJ prior without any data, which is useful for prior predictive checking. The generated quantities block reconstructs the actual correlation matrix from its Cholesky factor using Stan’s specialized function for computing \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\).

22.5.2 Understanding the Stan Types

The cholesky_factor_corr[K] type declares a \(K \times K\) lower triangular matrix L with special constraints. The diagonal elements must be strictly positive, and each row must have unit Euclidean norm. These constraints ensure that \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\) is a valid correlation matrix with ones on the diagonal. Stan handles the internal transformations needed to sample in an unconstrained space while maintaining these constraints.

The cholesky_factor_cov[K] type similarly declares a \(K \times K\) lower triangular matrix with positive diagonal elements, but without the unit norm constraint on rows. This type is appropriate for general covariance matrices where diagonal elements can take any positive values.

Stan provides several key functions for working with Cholesky factors. The lkj_corr_cholesky(eta) function specifies the LKJ prior on the Cholesky factor of a correlation matrix. The multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L) function efficiently computes \(\mathbf{L}\mathbf{L}^T\). The diag_pre_multiply(d, L) function computes \(\text{diag}(\mathbf{d}) \times \mathbf{L}\), which is useful for combining scale and correlation parameters. The cholesky_decompose(A) function computes the Cholesky factor of matrix A, though this is rarely needed since parameters are usually directly declared as Cholesky factors.

22.5.3 Combining Scale and Correlation

A common and recommended pattern separates the modeling of standard deviations from correlations:

data {
  int<lower=1> K;
}

parameters {
  vector<lower=0>[K] sigma;          // Standard deviations
  cholesky_factor_corr[K] L_Omega;   // Cholesky factor of correlation matrix
}

transformed parameters {
  cholesky_factor_cov[K] L_Sigma;    // Cholesky factor of covariance matrix
  L_Sigma = diag_pre_multiply(sigma, L_Omega);  // L_Sigma = diag(sigma) * L_Omega
}

model {
  sigma ~ cauchy(0, 2.5);            // Half-Cauchy prior on scales
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2.0);  // LKJ prior on correlations
}

generated quantities {
  corr_matrix[K] Omega;
  cov_matrix[K] Sigma;
  
  Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
  Sigma = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Sigma);
}

The mathematical relationship underlying this parameterization is elegant. A covariance matrix can be written as \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{\Omega}\mathbf{D}^T\), where D is the diagonal matrix of standard deviations and Ω is the correlation matrix. Applying the Cholesky decomposition to Ω gives \(\mathbf{\Omega} = \mathbf{L}_\Omega\mathbf{L}_\Omega^T\), so \(\mathbf{\Sigma} = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega\mathbf{L}_\Omega^T\mathbf{D}^T = (\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega)(\mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega)^T = \mathbf{L}_\Sigma\mathbf{L}_\Sigma^T\), where \(\mathbf{L}_\Sigma = \mathbf{D}\mathbf{L}_\Omega\). Stan’s diag_pre_multiply(sigma, L_Omega) efficiently computes this product.

22.6 Complete Bayesian Model: Multivariate Normal Estimation

Let’s implement a complete model for estimating the mean vector and covariance matrix of multivariate normal data, which forms the foundation for many more complex models.

22.6.1 Stan Model

data {
  int<lower=1> N;              // Number of observations
  int<lower=1> K;              // Number of variables
  vector[K] y[N];              // N observations, each K-dimensional
}

parameters {
  vector[K] mu;                // Mean vector
  vector<lower=0>[K] sigma;    // Standard deviations
  cholesky_factor_corr[K] L_Omega;  // Cholesky factor of correlation
}

transformed parameters {
  cholesky_factor_cov[K] L_Sigma;
  L_Sigma = diag_pre_multiply(sigma, L_Omega);
}

model {
  // Priors
  mu ~ normal(0, 10);
  sigma ~ cauchy(0, 2.5);
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2.0);
  
  // Likelihood
  y ~ multi_normal_cholesky(mu, L_Sigma);
}

generated quantities {
  corr_matrix[K] Omega;
  cov_matrix[K] Sigma;
  
  Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
  Sigma = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Sigma);
}

The prior specifications here are intentionally weakly informative. The normal prior on the mean components has large variance, allowing the data to dominate. The half-Cauchy prior on standard deviations is a robust choice that avoids placing too much mass near zero while remaining proper. The LKJ prior with \(\eta = 2\) expresses mild skepticism about strong correlations without being overly restrictive.

22.7 Example: Multivariate Regression Model

Now let’s implement a multivariate regression where we model multiple correlated outcomes simultaneously. This framework is particularly relevant for computational health research where patients often have multiple related health outcomes measured concurrently.

22.7.1 The Model

Suppose we have \(p\) outcome variables and \(q\) predictors. For observation \(i\), the model specifies:

\[ \mathbf{y}_i \sim N(\mathbf{X}_i\mathbf{B}, \mathbf{\Sigma}) \]

Here y_i is a \(p\)-dimensional outcome vector, X_i is a \(q\)-dimensional predictor vector (including an intercept), B is a \(q \times p\) matrix of regression coefficients, and Σ is the \(p \times p\) residual covariance matrix. The key advantage of this multivariate formulation is that the residual covariance Σ captures correlations among outcomes after accounting for the predictors. These residual correlations often reveal important biological or behavioral relationships that shared predictors don’t fully explain.

Consider a practical example from cardiovascular research. We might simultaneously model systolic blood pressure, diastolic blood pressure, and heart rate as functions of age, body mass index, and treatment status. While these predictors explain much of the variation in each outcome, the three cardiovascular measures remain intrinsically correlated through shared physiological mechanisms. The multivariate model captures this residual dependence, providing more accurate inference and enabling tests of hypotheses about the joint effects of interventions.

22.7.2 Stan Model

data {
  int<lower=1> N;              // Number of observations
  int<lower=1> P;              // Number of outcomes
  int<lower=1> Q;              // Number of predictors
  matrix[N, Q] X;              // Predictor matrix
  vector[P] y[N];              // Outcome vectors
}

parameters {
  matrix[Q, P] B;              // Regression coefficients (Q x P matrix)
  vector<lower=0>[P] sigma;    // Residual standard deviations
  cholesky_factor_corr[P] L_Omega;  // Cholesky factor of residual correlation
}

transformed parameters {
  cholesky_factor_cov[P] L_Sigma;
  L_Sigma = diag_pre_multiply(sigma, L_Omega);
}

model {
  // Priors
  to_vector(B) ~ normal(0, 5);       // Flat-ish prior on coefficients
  sigma ~ cauchy(0, 2.5);            // Half-Cauchy on scales
  L_Omega ~ lkj_corr_cholesky(2.0);  // Weakly informative LKJ prior
  
  // Likelihood
  for (n in 1:N) {
    y[n] ~ multi_normal_cholesky(X[n] * B, L_Sigma);
  }
}

generated quantities {
  corr_matrix[P] Omega;
  cov_matrix[P] Sigma;
  
  Omega = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Omega);
  Sigma = multiply_lower_tri_self_transpose(L_Sigma);
}

The regression coefficient matrix B has rows corresponding to predictors and columns corresponding to outcomes. Each column therefore represents a separate regression equation, but all equations share the same residual correlation structure. The use of to_vector(B) in the prior specification vectorizes the entire coefficient matrix, applying the same normal prior to each element. This could be refined if domain knowledge suggests different prior scales for different coefficients.

22.7.4 Interpretation for Health Research

The regression coefficients tell the primary scientific story about how predictors affect each outcome. Age shows consistent positive effects across all three cardiovascular measures, though the magnitudes differ. BMI has complex effects—increasing both blood pressures but decreasing heart rate, potentially reflecting different physiological pathways. The treatment effectively reduces all three measures, suggesting broad cardiovascular benefits.

The residual correlations reveal structure not explained by the predictors. Systolic and diastolic blood pressure remain highly correlated (0.69) even after accounting for age, BMI, and treatment. This strong residual correlation suggests these measures share common unmeasured determinants, perhaps arterial stiffness, kidney function, or genetic factors. The weaker but still substantial correlation between blood pressures and heart rate (around 0.3-0.4) similarly points to shared physiological regulation.

These findings have practical implications for clinical research and practice. The strong residual correlations between systolic and diastolic pressure suggest that interventions targeting shared pathophysiology might be particularly effective. The correlation structure could also inform more efficient clinical trial designs using composite endpoints or multivariate power calculations. The residual dependence motivates investigation of unmeasured confounders that could be incorporated into future models or interventions.

22.7.5 Posterior Predictive Checking

# Generate posterior predictive samples
y_pred <- array(NA, dim = c(nrow(B_samples), N, P))

for (s in 1:nrow(B_samples)) {
  # Extract parameters for this MCMC sample
  B_s <- matrix(B_samples[s, , ], nrow = Q, ncol = P)
  Sigma_s <- matrix(extract(fit_mvr, pars = "Sigma")$Sigma[s, , ], nrow = P, ncol = P)
  
  # Predict for each observation
  for (i in 1:N) {
    mu_pred <- X[i, ] %*% B_s
    y_pred[s, i, ] <- mvrnorm(1, mu = mu_pred, Sigma = Sigma_s)
  }
}

# Compare observed vs predicted for first outcome (SBP)
y_pred_mean <- apply(y_pred[, , 1], 2, mean)
y_pred_lower <- apply(y_pred[, , 1], 2, quantile, 0.025)
y_pred_upper <- apply(y_pred[, , 1], 2, quantile, 0.975)

ppc_df <- data.frame(
  observed = Y[, 1],
  predicted = y_pred_mean,
  lower = y_pred_lower,
  upper = y_pred_upper
)

ggplot(ppc_df, aes(x = predicted, y = observed)) +
  geom_point(alpha = 0.5) +
  geom_abline(slope = 1, intercept = 0, color = "red", linetype = "dashed") +
  geom_errorbar(aes(ymin = lower, ymax = upper), alpha = 0.2) +
  labs(title = "Posterior Predictive Check: Systolic BP",
       x = "Predicted SBP (posterior mean)",
       y = "Observed SBP") +
  theme_minimal()

The posterior predictive check visualizes model fit by comparing observed data to predictions from the fitted model. Points should cluster around the diagonal line representing perfect prediction. The error bars show 95% posterior predictive intervals, which should contain most observed values. Systematic deviations from the diagonal or high rates of observations falling outside the intervals would suggest model misspecification.

22.8 Connecting to Your Research

In propensity score models with multiple treatments, correlated treatment assignments often arise in clinical practice. For instance, combination therapies or sequential treatment decisions create dependencies that naive approaches ignore. Modeling the joint propensity through a multivariate probit using Cholesky factors provides more efficient and statistically valid estimates than fitting separate models and assuming independence.

Hierarchical models in multi-site studies with random effects across sites, hospitals, or geographic regions, the correlation structure between different random effect components (such as intercepts and slopes) contains important scientific information about heterogeneity. The Cholesky parameterization not only enables efficient estimation of this structure but also improves MCMC sampling efficiency dramatically compared to alternative approaches, which becomes critical when the number of random effects is large.

Longitudinal outcomes present another natural application domain. When applying sophisticated causal inference methods like TMLE or G-estimation to multiple correlated outcomes measured over time, the Cholesky parameterization allows efficient joint modeling while maintaining computational tractability. This joint approach can improve precision of effect estimates and enable testing of hypotheses about differential treatment effects across outcomes.

The overarching advantage across all these applications is computational efficiency. Cholesky factors transform \(O(n^3)\) matrix operations into \(O(n^2)\) operations, and Stan’s automatic differentiation works far more efficiently with the Cholesky parameterization than with full covariance matrices. For your work with large-scale health databases containing hundreds of thousands of patients across multiple sites with complex random effect structures, this efficiency translates directly into the feasibility of models that would otherwise be computationally intractable.
